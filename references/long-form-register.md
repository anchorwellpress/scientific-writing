# Long-Form Register

For book chapters, substantive essays, trade journalism, and Substack-style pieces where evidence supports a narrative argument.

The distinction from peer-reviewed register is not citation laxness. It is structural. Peer-reviewed papers are built around evidence; long-form pieces are built around argument, with evidence as scaffolding.

## Structure: Narrative Spine

The piece has a thesis and walks the reader through it. Evidence appears where it earns its place.

Standard long-form architecture:

1. **Opening scene or claim** — a specific case, observation, or sharp claim that grounds the piece. Citation rare; the opening is the writer's hand.
2. **The frame** — what this piece is actually about. Often surprising. The reader should know within the first 400–600 words what argument they are committing to.
3. **The substantiation** — evidence sections. This is where citations cluster. Each section advances one move in the argument.
4. **The complication** — counterargument, alternative framing, limit of the thesis. Treated honestly, not as a strawman.
5. **The resolution** — what the argument implies. Not a "call to action" in the activist sense; a "here is what changes if this is right" sense.

A chapter or essay lacking a complication section is either too short or too lazy. Add it.

## Tone

Translation register, dialed toward narrative. Voice closer to the better long-form magazine writing than to peer-reviewed prose.

What changes from peer-reviewed register:
- Specific cases (anonymized) appear in the body, not relegated to a sidebar.
- Personal observation is allowed throughout, marked as such by voice rather than by a Methods section.
- Hedging is calibrated to the reader's stake, not the journal reviewer's.

What does not change:
- Translation register holds. No condescension. No popularizer flattening.
- No memoir-effusive drift. The writer's experience appears in service of the argument, not as the argument.
- Authority through what the sentence shows, not through credentialing claims.

## Citation Density and Placement

Lower density than peer-reviewed, higher selectivity. Three types:

1. **Load-bearing citations** — the piece's argument depends on this fact being true. Verify rigorously. Vancouver-formatted endnotes or footnotes.
2. **Texture citations** — supporting context, named studies, datasets. Verify, but tolerance for brief reference is higher.
3. **Voice-of-the-field citations** — naming a thinker or a school of thought. Often a book or essay rather than a paper. Vancouver still applies.

Rule of thumb: if a claim is surprising or contestable, cite it. If it is general knowledge in the field, do not. Calibrate to the reader, not to the field.

## Format Considerations

For book chapters:
- Endnotes by chapter, Vancouver style. Not in-text superscripts (visual noise in trade prose).
- A "Sources and Further Reading" section per chapter is appropriate. A full reference list in the back is standard.

For Substack and standalone articles:
- 2,000–4,000 words is typical
- One thesis per piece
- Citations as endnotes or hyperlinks. If the publication supports hyperlinks, use them and provide a "Sources" section at the end with Vancouver-formatted full references.
- Read-aloud test: a long-form piece should sustain interest when read aloud. Sentences too dense to read aloud are usually too dense.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

1. **Evidence-as-edifice rather than evidence-as-scaffold.** Trade readers do not want a literature review. They want an argument, with evidence in the right places.
2. **Citation-stuffing the opening.** The first paragraph almost never benefits from a citation.
3. **Lecture register.** Translation requires a reader-in-mind. Lecture register addresses the field, not the reader.
4. **Outrage register.** Cheap and dilutes the analysis. Restraint is more persuasive.
5. **Anecdote-as-evidence.** A specific case opens the piece; it does not carry the argument alone.
6. **Pop-inspirational closing.** No "we can do better." End with a specific implication or a sharp observation.

## Pre-Publication Checklist

- [ ] Thesis stated explicitly within first 600 words
- [ ] Complication section exists and is honest
- [ ] Every load-bearing citation passes Gate 2 (verification) and Gate 4 (fact match)
- [ ] Endnotes or hyperlinks formatted consistently
- [ ] Read-aloud test passes on opening and closing 400 words
